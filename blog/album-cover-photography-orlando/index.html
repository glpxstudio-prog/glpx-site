Album Cover Photography in Orlando

By Gerson Lopez · GLPX Studio, Orlando FL

An album cover is a square that has to work at sixty pixels wide.

That is the whole problem. On Spotify, on Apple Music, in someone's recently played list, your cover is the size of a fingernail. It also has to hold up as a poster, on merch and on a billboard if the single does what you want it to do. Very few photos survive both ends of that.

I have shot covers and press work for artists including Alex Rose and Anuel AA. Here is how these shoots actually get planned.

Start with the deliverables, not the location

Before anyone talks about where to shoot, we decide what we are making. A cover shoot usually needs:

  • The cover. Square, tight, one clear subject, readable tiny.
  • Press photos. Horizontal, with negative space on one side so a magazine or blog can drop text over it.
  • Socials. Vertical, 9 by 16, framed so nothing important sits where the caption and buttons cover the screen.
  • Alternates. Labels and DSPs ask for variations more often than artists expect. Canvas loops, a clean version without a logo, a version that works on a dark background.

Those are four different crops. A shoot planned for one of them and cropped into the others always looks it.

The small size test

Anything busy dies at thumbnail size. Thin text, fine patterns, three people at equal distance, a face lost in a wide landscape, all gone.

What survives is contrast, a strong silhouette, and one thing the eye lands on. So we shoot tighter than the reference images usually suggest, and I check every selected frame scaled down to a hundred pixels before we call it. If it does not read at that size it is a press photo, not a cover.

Concept before camera

The best covers I have shot came from a conversation about the music, not about photography.

What is the record about. Is it a victory lap or a confession. Does it sound expensive or does it sound like a basement. Should the artist look at the camera or past it. Dark or bright. Those answers decide lighting and wardrobe faster than any mood board.

Bring the music if you have it, even rough. Fifteen seconds of a mix tells me more than an hour of reference photos.

Studio or Orlando locations

A studio session in Orlando is the right call for a controlled cover. Hard light, color gels, a seamless background, smoke, whatever the concept needs. Nothing in the frame is there by accident. For anything that has to look deliberate and expensive, this is where we start.

On location brings the city in. Downtown Orlando glass and concrete for something harder. Mills 50 murals for color. Thornton Park brick for something warmer. Lake Eola at night when you want the skyline. We usually pair a studio half with a location half so the set has range without needing two shoot days.

What artists should bring

  • More wardrobe than you think. Three to five looks. Jewelry, hats, glasses, anything you actually wear.
  • A steamer or at least a plan. Wrinkles are the most common thing I have to fix in post.
  • One person whose opinion you trust. Not six. A crowded set slows everything down.
  • Your release date. It sets the retouching schedule.

Hair and makeup is optional and worth it for anything that will live on a cover for years. I bring in an artist when the shoot calls for it, billed at their rate.

Rights and usage

Tell me up front where the images are going. A cover and a paid campaign are different usage, and it belongs in the quote rather than being discovered later. Label deliverable requirements also tend to arrive as a PDF with exact pixel sizes, so send it over before the shoot and I will build the shot list around it.
